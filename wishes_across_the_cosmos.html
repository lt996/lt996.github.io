01 — The Design Challenge

Valentine's events hand players a rose. We handed them an empty bottle.

Valentine's is the highest-emotional-stakes beat on an otome calendar — and the most formulaic. The default shape is consumption: log in, claim the date scene, read the holiday story. The player is the audience of a romantic gesture, never its author.

The genre default

Romance as content delivery

Holiday events typically deliver pre-authored intimacy: scripted dates, gift boxes, costumes. It works — but every player receives the same gesture, and receiving is a passive verb. The emotional ceiling is set by the writers, not the player.

The opportunity

Romance as something you make

A gift you assembled yourself carries a different weight than one you were issued — effort and choice are what make a gesture feel like yours. The design bet: players would invest more emotion in a Valentine's gift they built, from materials they personally gathered, than in any scene we could script.

The design question

Can a LiveOps event make the player the author of the romantic gesture — with enough expressive range that no two gifts look alike, and enough systemic warmth that every gift feels seen?

02 — The Event Loop

Gather under starlight, craft by hand, give it to him

The loop is built so that the gift's ingredients are the gameplay's output — by the time the player seals the bottle, every grain of sand inside it is something they went out and collected, with him along for the trip.

Gameplay

Collect star sand

Navigate 12 hand-designed levels — path planning, traps, and obstacles — to gather star sand, while an AI-driven companion reacts to the run.

Creation

Craft the bottle

Layer the DIY: bottle silhouette, sand colors, inner ornaments, flowing oil, shimmer powder, and the seal on top.

Recognition

The bottle is read

The finished bottle receives poetic comment lines — the system responds to the creation like a person would, not like a scoreboard.

Emotional payoff

Gift it to him

The player sends the bottle to their chosen male lead — closing the loop the way Valentine's should: with a gesture, given.

The collection stage matters more than it looks. I designed all 12 levels — route choices, trap placement, obstacle pacing — plus the companion's behavior logic, so he's present while you gather: warning you to take it slow in dangerous corners, reacting as you go. So the materials aren't a resource grind; they're souvenirs of time spent together. The bottle becomes physical evidence of shared time before it's ever decorated.

03 — The Crafting System

Six layers of authorship, one bottle

Expressive range was the core spec. Each crafting layer is an independent decision axis, so the combination space is large enough that a player's bottle functions as a signature — and the four male leads each have a reference bottle in their own visual language to anchor inspiration without constraining it.

Layer 1 · 瓶子

Bottle silhouette

The base form — flask, sphere, crystal-stoppered perfume bottle — sets the overall character of the piece.

Layer 2 · 星沙

Star sand

The collected material, in multiple colors. Layered fills create gradients — the player's gathered effort, made visible.

Layer 3 · 内饰

Inner ornaments

Miniature scenes inside the glass: stars, butterflies, blossoms — the storytelling layer.

Layer 4 · 流沙油

Flowing oil

Adds motion — sand that drifts and settles when the bottle moves, so the keepsake feels alive.

Layer 5 · 浮光粉

Shimmer powder

The finishing light pass: glint and sparkle tuned to the bottle's palette.

Layer 6 · 封口

The seal

Ribbon and stopper — the gesture that turns a craft project into a gift. Sealing is the commitment moment.

Why independent layers instead of presets: presets produce identical gifts, which would collapse the event's premise. Six independent axes mean the system can offer guidance (reference bottles, palettes) while guaranteeing that what comes out is the player's own — important both for the emotional claim and for what happened next on social media.
04 — The Design Decisions

Three calls that made it a gift, not a craft minigame

i.

Materials carry memory

The sand inside the bottle is sand the player personally collected, with the male lead present and talking throughout the run. Gameplay output becomes gift input — so the finished bottle isn't an item from a menu, it's compressed evidence of time spent together. This single connection is what separates "DIY feature" from "romantic gesture."

星沙是和他一起收集的 — 礼物里装的是共同度过的时间。
ii.

Witnessed, never graded

A finished bottle receives poetic comment lines — "Brilliant as stars, and as eternal" — rather than a score, a star rating, or a rarity tier. The moment a creation is graded, players optimize instead of express; evaluation kills authorship. The system's job here is to be the first person who sees the gift and says something kind about it.

星愿瓶只有评语，没有评分 — 系统是第一个看见这份礼物的人。
iii.

Built to leave the game

The display framing, lighting, and poetic captions make every finished bottle a complete, screenshot-ready composition. That isn't cosmetic polish — it's distribution design. A Valentine's gift players are proud of is a Valentine's gift players post, and every shared bottle is organic reach the event didn't have to buy.

展示界面按"值得截图分享"设计 — 玩家的骄傲就是活动的传播。

The thesis underneath all three: in an otome, the strongest emotional content is not what the game shows the player — it's what the game lets the player mean.

最强的情感内容，是玩家被允许表达的那一份
05 — Seen In Game

The loop, from sand to seal

① Collect

One of the 12 collection levels — planning a route through traps and obstacles to gather star sand. He's there the whole time — "It's dangerous here. Take it slow." The materials arrive already attached to a memory.

② Craft

The DIY bench: bottle, contents, seal as separate tabs; star sand, inner ornaments, flowing oil, and shimmer powder as a palette. Every panel is one authorship decision.

③ Anchor

Each of the four male leads has a reference bottle in his own visual language — "start crafting the bottle you want to give him." Inspiration with a direction, not a template to copy.

④ Witness & give

The finished bottle, framed and captioned by the system — "Brilliant as stars, and as eternal" · "Every past and future we hold shines like this light" — with one button left: send it to him.

06 — Player Validation

Players turned their bottles into posts

The clearest validation for an expression system isn't a rating — it's whether players treat the output as worth showing other people.

No two alike. Player-shared galleries like this one — eighteen bottles, eighteen distinct compositions — are the combination space doing exactly what it was specified to do: every gift reads as a signature.
Sharing was voluntary distribution. Players curated grids of their own creations and posted them to social platforms unprompted. An event about giving a gift to a character ended with players giving the event itself an audience.
The display design paid off. Bottles were posted as-shot from the display screen — framing, light, and captions intact — confirming that "screenshot-ready" was the right bar for the presentation layer.
What I'd claim, and what I wouldn't: organic sharing volume is a qualitative signal of expressive success, not a conversion metric — I wouldn't attribute revenue to it without proper tracking. What it does demonstrate is that players valued their own output enough to publish it, which is the precise emotional outcome the event was designed around.
07 — Key Takeaways

What this event taught me as a designer

i.

Authorship is an emotional multiplier

The same bottle, granted from a gacha, would mean nothing. Effort, choice, and ownership are what convert content into feeling — and they're systems decisions, not art decisions.

ii.

Connect the loop's ends and the middle carries itself

Because collection fed creation and creation fed the relationship, no stage needed external incentives to feel worth doing. The weakest event loops are chains of separately-rewarded chores.

iii.

Feedback systems set the creative temperature

Comments instead of scores told players "express" rather than "optimize." How a system responds to player output determines what kind of output players make.

iv.

Design the screenshot, not just the screen

Treating shareability as a first-class spec — not a marketing afterthought — turned the event's most emotional moment into its distribution channel.

Teahouse Gossip removed pressure so players could rest. Wishes Across the Cosmos removed the script so players could speak. Different events, same conviction: the system should serve the feeling.
