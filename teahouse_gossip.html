01 — The Design Challenge

The genre says hurry. The product says stay.

The brief was a one-week event delivering low-pressure daily emotional content. The obvious vehicle — a sim-management serving minigame — came with a problem baked into the genre itself.

The market pattern

Time pressure is the default

Sim-management minigames in mobile games almost universally run on countdown mechanics — order timers, patience meters, shift clocks. The anxiety response this produces is well documented, and players arrive pre-conditioned: anyone who has played a cooking or serving game expects to be rushed before the first guest sits down.

The product philosophy

Companionship and relaxation

World Beyond's core promise to its players is companionship — a place to decompress, not perform. A timer-driven minigame wouldn't just be off-brand; it would directly contradict the reason players open the game at all.

The design question

Take the genre's most anxiety-coded format and rebuild it so that relaxation is enforced by the systems themselves — while still giving players a real puzzle worth solving and a narrative reason to come back six days in a row.

02 — The Core Mechanic

Every guest is a riddle. Every cup is the answer.

Players run a small teahouse. Guests describe what they want — but never directly. The game replaces time pressure with deduction: a different kind of engagement that asks for attention instead of speed.

Step 1

Read the guest

Each guest hints at their order in vague, indirect language across three dimensions at once.

听客人描述
Step 2

Decode the recipe

Cross-reference the in-game Tea Compendium to translate hints into a concrete combination.

查阅茶典
Step 3

Boil · brew · serve

Pick a base, add ingredients, grind or not, pour. The boiling pause is ritual, not countdown.

Step 4

Earn the gossip

A satisfied guest leans in and shares a rumor about one of the male leads — the event's real reward.

03 — The Design Philosophy

"No pressure" engineered as three system layers

It would have been easy to make the event merely look calm — soft music, warm palette — while keeping punishing systems underneath. Instead, tolerance was implemented at every operational level a player can touch.

i.

Result tolerance — failure costs nothing

A wrong combination triggers a gentle response — "This doesn't quite suit my taste — could you try again?" — and the player simply retries. No penalty, no consumed attempt, and crucially, the gossip reward is never blocked by failure. Wrong answers are feedback, not punishment.

结果容错 — 失败无惩罚，奖励永不因出错而锁定。
System guarantee: every session always ends in success
ii.

Rhythm tolerance — time belongs to the player

No action in the event has a time limit. The one place where waiting exists — the few seconds of water coming to a boil — is deliberately framed as ritual pacing: it makes you feel like you're actually brewing tea, rather than racing a meter. The same seconds that a cooking game weaponizes into urgency become, here, the texture of the fantasy.

节奏容错 — 烧水的等待是仪式感，不是倒计时。
System guarantee: zero countdown surfaces anywhere in the event
iii.

Operation tolerance — order never matters

Tea base, additives, hot water — add them in any sequence and the result is identical. This removes an entire category of hidden failure (sequence memorization) so the player's full attention stays on the one thing that is actually interesting: decoding the recipe.

操作容错 — 投放顺序不影响结果，专注解谜本身。
System guarantee: only the combination is evaluated, never the sequence

The three layers add up to one design value: "no pressure" is not a tone — it is a system property. If any single layer had been skipped, the other two would have read as decoration.

「无压力」不是氛围，是系统属性
04 — Narrative Integration

The teahouse is the setup. The visit is the payoff.

The brewing game was never meant to stand alone. Its real job is emotional priming — manufacturing curiosity that the story content then resolves.

Gameplay

Run the teahouse

Spend an action point, serve three guests, solve three small puzzles.

Reward

Collect the gossip

Two of the three guests share male-lead-related rumors — fragments, not full stories.

Emotional content

Visit him

The player brings the rumor to the male lead it concerns — already curious, already invested.

Closure

Uncover the truth

Exclusive event dialogue resolves the gossip — confirming, subverting, or reframing it.

The sequencing is the point. By the time the player reaches the male lead, they have already been primed with a question they want answered. The visit feels like following up on something they care about — not collecting a quest reward. Gameplay manufactures the curiosity; narrative spends it.

A structural choice that protects the casual player: the 12 pieces of gossip (3 per male lead) are independent storylines, not a progressive chain. Miss a day, and nothing downstream is locked or diminished — each rumor resolves on its own. Progression pressure was deliberately kept out of the narrative structure for the same reason timers were kept out of the gameplay: a six-day "low-pressure" event that punishes a skipped day isn't low-pressure.
05 — Seen In Game

One full loop, as the player meets it

① The riddle

A guest gives her clues — "golden soup to match golden flowers" (color → Yellow tea) and "the leaves spoil the look, process them for me" (form → grind to powder). Three decision dimensions, delivered in character, never as a checklist.

② The reference

The Tea Compendium 茶典 — always one tap away. Each entry maps a finished tea to its color, flavor profile, and steps, turning every order from a memory test into a cross-referencing deduction.

③ The reward

The tea lands, and the guest leans in: "I know a big secret. I'll only tell you — don't pass it on." The mechanical reward and the narrative hook are the same object.

06 — Player Validation

Players named the design decision themselves

Community discussion volume during the event ran notably higher than comparable events of the same tier. But the more interesting evidence is qualitative — what players chose to praise, unprompted. Quotes translated from Chinese.

07 — Key Takeaways

What this event taught me as a designer

i.

A design value has to survive contact with every system

"Relaxing" was only true because result handling, pacing, and input rules all enforced it independently. Values that live in art direction alone get falsified by the first punishing mechanic.

ii.

Removing pressure requires replacing it with substance

A timer-less serving game with nothing else is just slow. The deduction layer — three clue dimensions, one compendium, solvable puzzles — is what made calm engaging rather than empty.

iii.

LiveOps gameplay should manufacture the appetite its narrative satisfies

Gossip-as-reward made the story visit feel self-motivated. The strongest event loops don't alternate gameplay and story; they make one create demand for the other.

iv.

Genre conventions are defaults, not requirements

The countdown timer survives in sim-management games because nobody removes it — not because players want it. The loudest player praise went to a feature that, mechanically, was an absence.

Every decision in Teahouse Gossip answers the same question: does this make the player feel rushed, or received? What I'd bring to a LiveOps team is the habit of treating an emotional promise as a systems spec — and auditing every mechanic against it.
